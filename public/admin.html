How these are graded

Typical trip: our number inside the middle half of Google's list (cheapest and dearest quarters dropped). Fewer than 4 prices: within ±10% of their middle.

Cheapest trip: within ±10% of the cheapest listed. Disney hotels: the cheapest in the category the traveler picked. Off property: the cheapest in each price third.

Overall: both together. Lean: our typical fare against the middle of Google's list (+ = high).

Flights use the number written down before each fare was bought. Disney hotels are graded with that hotel and month's own Google rates left out of our correction. Off property, each search is graded by the one before.

Colors: green 60%+ (about the best the noise allows: the same flight is within 10% of itself on only ~67% of days), amber 40-59%, red under 40%.

How to read this

Bought: fares we paid Google for, last 90 days. Free feed: the free flight feed, last 90 days. Google lists: Google's whole answers we kept. Survey: the government's (BTS) quarterly / monthly surveys held for the route; it covers US flights only.

Nightly job, ~date: one of the 19 airports the nightly job rotates through (about 8 routes a night), so the date is a rough guess. Not in rotation: one of the other 22 airports. Buy it once by naming it in the popular-routes workflow's routes box (for example CLT-HND-2027-03), or screenshot a fare into Prices you've checked. Waiting on the government: we hold fares but no monthly survey yet.

Hotels: the nightly job searches about 6 resort-months a night, longest-waiting first, never one searched in the last 10 days, and a different night each time. Google won't price a month more than ~360 days out.

How close our flight estimates are

Every night we buy about 18 real Google Flights fares, each the middle-priced flight for one route and date. Just before buying each one we write down what a traveler would have seen and what the candidate formula would have said. Those are the numbers at the top of this page: nothing could have learned from the fare it is graded on, so they are the honest answer to “how far off are we right now?” Every day they should get better; the two-week table says whether they are, beyond luck.

Accurate means within ±10% of the real fare, in either direction: $100 low and $100 high are the same miss. (Change the ± in Rates & settings, flight.zonePct; it only changes the grading.)

Checked before buying: how far off are we right now

Every night, just before buying each fare, we write down the number a traveler would have seen for that route and date at that moment, with everything the formula had learned so far. Then we buy the fare and compare. Nothing here could have learned from the fare it is graded on, so this is the honest answer to “how far off is the number on screen?” It started with the 2026-10-08 run, grows by about 18 fares a night, and can't be rebuilt for nights before it started.

Airfares, by the government's price indexes

From the Bureau of Labor Statistics, fetched weekly. They say how fares MOVED across the country, never what one route costs. The board shows travelers the first line. The candidate formula uses the consumer index to bring older route surveys up to today and the international index for older fares, each only as far as our bought fares show it helps (the coverage report has the before/after).

After learning: live vs candidate (the live column is flattered)

Graded on every fare we hold, AFTER the live formula has already learned from it, so its column looks kinder than the truth. The candidate is graded leave-one-out. Use the before-buying numbers above to judge; this table is here to see more fares at once.

Which lean fits best

“Lean” is set separately for US and international routes in Rates & settings (flight.estimateLean and flight.estimateLeanIntl; international follows the US one until you set it): 0 shows the cheap end of a route's usual range, 50 the middle, 100 the dear end. Graded on the formula before any bought fare corrects the route, because that is the part the lean moves. Only matters while travelers see the live formula; the candidate isn't leaned. “Typically off by” is how far a typical estimate missed; the smallest number in each row is the best fit. Leans: which way the middle fare tilts (high = our number above the real fare); it says nothing about how close the rest are.

Routes, furthest off first (by what travelers see now)

Honest limits: “shown now” is partly graded by the same fares it learned from, so it reads a little kinder than a route we've never bought; the candidate is graded only on fares it did not learn from. A route with 1 or 2 fares is a hint, not a verdict.

How close our hotel numbers are

Hotels have no single real price we buy, so this grades them three ways, by the same rule as flights: accurate means within ±10% of the real price, in either direction. + means our number was above the real one.

Disney's own hotels, priced by Google

Our nightly “hotels near the resort” search also returns Disney's own hotels. Their real rate grades the Disney hotel prices we otherwise estimate, and (since 2026-10-03) also moves them, the same way your price checks do; the weight is in Rates & settings → Data sources. Both are before taxes and fees, for the same night or month. “Shown now” is what travelers see, after your checks and Google's rates adjust it, so it is partly graded by the same Google rates.

Off-property: what we showed, then the next real price

An off-property rate is one real night stretched across the month until the next search about 10 days later. This compares what we were showing for that next night with what Google then said. History starts with this update, so it fills in over the next couple of weeks.

What Google tells us beyond the price: usual prices and booking sites

Every hotel search also says, per hotel, what each booking site charges and, when tonight is a deal, Google's “less than usual” note. Since 2026-10-12 both are used: we price from the cheapest place to book, and a deal night is blended toward Google's usual price by a weight the record teaches (hotels Google showed us twice for the same month: which would have predicted the later price better?). It starts near 0 and grows only as the pairs back it. The same price feeds Google's rates for Disney's own hotels above.

International: what we bought vs our starting guess

Every international estimate starts from one flat guess ($754 to Europe, $1,087 to Asia, the same from every US city), bent by a made-up season shape, then multiplied by one correction learned from the fares we buy. If the destinations below sit far apart, one shared correction can't be right for all of them. "x1.60" means real fares ran 1.6 times the starting guess.

By travel season

The starting guess says July–September is 37% dearer than spring. If real fares disagree, summer trips overseas read high.

Our fare vs Google's "typical price"

Two different questions. Our fare is the middle-priced flight for one specific date. Google's typical price is what the cheapest seat on the route usually costs across the year, often a budget airline on a quiet date. Ours sitting above Google's typical range is expected, not an error.

Flight demand vs our crowd levels

For each paid search, Google says whether prices are low, typical or high for that time. Here they are by resort and travel month, beside our crowd level for the same month. Where "high" piles up in a month we call quiet (or the other way round), that's a month worth a second look. A handful of searches proves nothing; read the count first.

Government fare surveys we hold

US routes start from the government's survey of what people actually paid. The quarterly survey ended in June 2025; its monthly replacement is being added. Older quarters are kept, never replaced.

Indicator study: what actually moves a fare

Read only. Every paid fare we've bought goes into one regression (a ridge regression on the log of the fare, so every effect reads as a percentage). It answers two questions: which indicators really move the price, and does knowing them make us more accurate on fares the model never saw? Nothing here changes a price.

  • Focus ranks indicators by how much they move fares across the range we actually see. Start at the top.
  • Solid means 200 refits on resampled fares agree on the direction; maybe mostly agree; not yet means the data can't tell. "Can't tell yet" means our fares don't vary on it.
  • Accuracy is measured fairly: each fare is predicted by a model that was fitted without it.

Weekday, booking ahead and airlines: where they stand

  • Day of week is in the study ("Leaving Friday–Sunday" above) and in the candidate formula, which now takes it from the free feed (the same route and month on different days, so route and season cancel): on 3,976 fares as of 2026-10-12, every weekday was within 2% of the others. Paid fares used to all leave on the 15th, which mixed weekday up with month; since 2026-10-12 they rotate across the 8th–22nd by route.
  • Booking a few weeks out: "How far ahead it was searched" above covers the months-ahead range we buy in. Google's price history for the same flight barely moves over the ~2 months it shows. The free feed can't answer this: it repeats the same stored price night after night.
  • Budget airlines and number of airlines on a route are read from the government's monthly survey since 2026-10-12 and are in both models above. The candidate formula also learns a budget-airline setting for US routes.

Prices you've checked

Every real price you bring in is kept, with the day you saw it and the dates it's for, next to what we were quoting for the same thing that day. Nothing here replaces a rate. A check that matches something we price (a flight route, a Disney hotel, a resort's tickets) nudges that estimate toward what you saw: one check a quarter of the way, three checks halfway. Restaurant items and hotels we don't price are kept as a record.

Ticket calendars

    
Disneyland: 1-day calendars only. Its 2-5 day tickets cost one price for any
      date, so the board already prices them at Disneyland's own figures. Its 1-day calendar is welcome
      but optional; it prices 1-day Disneyland trips.

    
Disney's own ticket prices, date by date. On Disney's ticket page pick a ticket length (say
      5 days), and screenshot the calendar that shows a price under every day, one month per
      screenshot. Drop them here, several at once if you like. Each month opens as a little calendar to check:
      fix any misread box, then save. From then on the board prices tickets on those dates from Disney's
      number.

    
Each ticket length is its own calendar. Disney's 2-day and 4-day prices for the same date are
      different, so import as many lengths per month as you like: a 4-day October never replaces a 2-day
      October. A trip of N park days uses the N-day calendar when you've imported it (Disney's exact price),
      otherwise the nearest length you have, adjusted by Disney's published length discounts.

    
Read on this device, like the other readers: the pictures aren't sent anywhere. The prices
      are Disney's “per day” price for adults (Ages 10+), before tax; the app adds the tax and works
      out the child price. Every import is kept; a newer one of the same month and length wins.

The attraction list

    
What each resort has, which is how the board can tell somebody that only Shanghai has Zootopia.
      The list below is what the app is using right now. The rows it shipped with are a starting point,
      not a researched catalogue — changing one here takes effect immediately, with no deploy.

    
List every resort that has it. “Only here” is never something you tick: it is worked out
      from how many resorts you name, so adding Paris to Ratatouille automatically stops the app claiming
      EPCOT is the only place to ride it. A ride with a different name abroad is still the same ride —
      put both resorts on one row and explain the difference in the note.

    
Lands and rides both belong here. Set the type to land for a whole themed area (Cars Land,
      Zootopia) and attraction for a ride or show. The land column says where it sits at each resort, as
      wdw: Liberty Square; dlr: New Orleans Square. Hide keeps a row but takes it off the
      traveler's list, so you choose what's shown without losing anything you typed.

    
Each resort's list of lands follows this list too. Hide a land and it comes off that resort's
      “features these lands” list. Add a land and give it a park, as
      wdw: Animal Kingdom, and it appears under that park; with no park it shows under
      “Also”. To rename a land, hide the old row and add a new one.

Remove a land that closed (like DinoLand)

        
Easiest: press Remove beside it on the Lands page. From here, press
          Hide on its row; if it has no row, add one with the land's exact name, the resort and Type Land,
          Save, then Hide it. Hidden rows fold away under “Hidden” at the bottom of the list.


        
Rename a land

        
Hide the old row, then add a new row with the new name (steps above). Editing the name in place
          doesn't work, because the app can't tell the new name was meant to replace the old one.


        
Move a land to a different park

        
Press Edit on the land's row, change Park (e.g. wdw: Hollywood Studios), and Save.


        
One land at several resorts

        
Use one row. Put every resort in Resorts (wdw dlr), and give each its park,
          separated by a semicolon: wdw: Magic Kingdom; dlr: Disneyland Park.


        
Say which land a ride is in

        
That goes on the ride's row, not the land's. Edit the ride, fill Land with
          wdw: Tomorrowland; shdr: Tomorrowland, and Save. The board then says
          “Space Mountain in Tomorrowland”.


        
Undo

        
Hidden rows are kept, folded away under “Hidden” at the bottom of the list. Press Show again on one to bring it back.


        
In the spreadsheet

        
The same three columns: type (land or attraction), land and
          park. For land and park, write each resort as resort: name and separate
          resorts with ;. Only the first colon counts, so Star Wars: Galaxy's Edge is fine.


        
Spelling: capitals and punctuation don't matter when the app matches names
          (Star Wars Galaxy's Edge matches Star Wars: Galaxy's Edge), and park names match loosely
          (Animal Kingdom finds Disney's Animal Kingdom). The words themselves must match.

Wait times we've recorded

    
Every two hours a job reads each park's posted waits from Queue-Times and keeps the ones
      taken between 9am and 7pm local time. Travelers never see this: posted waits run long, and not by
      the same amount at every resort. It's an archive, saved so there's a year of history to judge
      by when the time comes. A park showing nothing recorded, or nothing in the last day, is one to tell
      Claude about.
